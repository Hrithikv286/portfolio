Week 6 — Client Project UI/UX & Computational Embedded Hardware

Week 6 combined client-focused digital product design in Figma with hands-on embedded electronics. The week began with our Alpha team's first client meeting and a continuation of the UI/UX work on our project, and then moved into a computational hardware session covering transistor circuits, soldering practice, ESP32 programming, and the study of sensors and actuators. Together, these activities showed me how a complete product brings together both its digital interface and the electronic hardware behind it.

First Alpha Team Client Meeting

At the start of the week, I attended our Alpha team's first client meeting regarding our problem statement. The aim of the meeting was to establish the project requirements and define clear scope boundaries, so that the team understood exactly what the client expected and what would fall within or outside the scope of the project. We discussed the problem in detail, clarified the client's goals, and noted the key requirements that our solution would need to meet. This meeting gave our team a shared understanding of the project from the beginning and helped us plan our next steps with a clear direction.

UI/UX Continuation Session

Continuing from the UI/UX work in Week 5, we developed the digital side of our project in Figma. We first worked on the project branding, deciding on a visual identity for the application, such as its name, colour scheme, and overall style, so that the product would look consistent and recognisable. Next, we created a mind map to organise the different dimensions of the problem and connect them to possible solutions, which helped us think through the problem in a structured way. Using these ideas, we built a site map that defined the structure and flow of the application, showing how the different pages and screens connect to one another. We then designed the webpage layouts and created wireframes in Figma, which are simple outlines of each screen showing where content, buttons, and navigation elements are placed. Finally, we developed an interactive application prototype in Figma, linking the screens together so that a user could click through the application as if it were real. This prototype focused on the user flow and the UI/UX structure, allowing us to test whether the application was easy to understand and navigate before any development work begins.

Session: Computational Hardware & Embedded Systems

Later in the week, we attended a comprehensive session on computational hardware and embedded systems. The session covered digital electronics, transistor logic, soldering practice, microcontrollers, and the use of sensors and actuators, and it combined theory with practical, hands-on activities.

Electronics Basics & NOT Gate

We began with the basic concepts of electronics, including voltage, current, resistance, and how components such as resistors and transistors behave in a circuit. We then implemented a transistor-based inverter circuit, also known as a NOT gate, using an NPN transistor. In this circuit, when the input is HIGH, the transistor switches on and the output goes LOW, and when the input is LOW, the transistor switches off and the output goes HIGH. By studying the circuit diagram and wiring the components on a dot board, I learned how to read a schematic, place components correctly, and understand how transistors are used as electronic switches to build logic gates, which are the foundation of digital electronics.

Soldering & PCB Work

Next, we learned the fundamentals of soldering. We were taught how to control the temperature of the soldering iron so that the joint heats properly without damaging the components or the board. We practised soldering components onto PCB boards and also learned how to desolder components to remove or replace them. Through this practice, I learned how to make clean, strong, and reliable solder joints, and I understood the importance of safety, patience, and precision when working with electronic hardware.

Target Shooter ESP32 Game

As a practical project, we developed an interactive “Target Shooter” game using an ESP32 microcontroller. The game integrated an OLED display to show the game screen and targets, an analog joystick to control the aim, and an ultrasonic sensor to measure distance and add an extra element of interaction to the gameplay. Building this project helped me understand how a microcontroller reads inputs from different sensors, processes them in its program, and updates the output on a display in real time. It also showed me how hardware and programming come together to create an interactive embedded system.

Sensors & Actuators

For the final activity, I selected a PIR sensor (passive infrared motion sensor) and a door strike actuator (solenoid lock) and prepared a technical presentation explaining their working principles and applications. A PIR sensor detects motion by sensing changes in the infrared radiation emitted by warm bodies, such as people, within its field of view, and it is widely used in security systems, automatic lighting, and alarms. A door strike actuator uses a solenoid, which is an electromagnet, to lock or unlock a door when an electrical signal is applied, and it is commonly used in access control and smart security systems. Preparing this presentation improved my understanding of how sensors detect changes in their environment and how actuators turn electrical signals into physical action, and it also helped me practise explaining technical concepts clearly.

Overall, Week 6 was a valuable learning experience that combined digital product design with embedded hardware. The client meeting and the UI/UX work in Figma taught me how to turn client requirements into a structured and user-friendly application design, from branding and site maps to wireframes and an interactive prototype. The computational hardware session gave me hands-on experience with transistor logic, soldering, ESP32 programming, and sensors and actuators, strengthening my practical skills in electronics and embedded systems.
