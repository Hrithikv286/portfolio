Week 8 — Project Prototype, Robot Selection & Robot Operating System (ROS)

Week 8 consisted of finalising the electrical component architecture for our project prototype, a robot selection exercise built around an industrial scenario, and an intensive session on the Robot Operating System (ROS), Linux environments, movement simulation, and Gazebo modelling. The week connected the practical planning of our own prototype with an introduction to the software tools used to build, control, and simulate modern robotic systems.

Activity 1 — Project Prototype – Electrical Component Finalisation

During the first two days of the week, we worked on the development of our project prototype. For the electrical side of the project, we identified the components the prototype would need, evaluated the available options against the project's requirements, and finalised the electrical components needed for the upcoming hardware prototype. This involved thinking about how each component would fit into the overall system and how the parts would work together. Finalising the components at this stage gave our team a clear plan for the hardware build and helped make sure that the prototype would meet the requirements identified in our earlier client meetings.

Activity 2 — Robot Selection Activity

In this activity, we were given a set of robots and a defined industrial scenario, along with a budget of 100 points. Our task was to evaluate each robot against the requirements of the scenario and select the most suitable robot for the job. To do this, we compared the robots on factors such as cost, capability, and how well each one fitted the needs and constraints of the scenario, and weighed the trade-offs between them before making our final choice. This activity showed me that selecting a robot for an industrial application is not about choosing the most powerful or most advanced option, but about finding the best balance between performance, cost, and suitability for the specific task within the available budget.

Session: ROS – Robot Operating System

Later in the week, we attended a dedicated session on the Robot Operating System (ROS). The session explored robotics communication frameworks, joint kinematics, Linux workflows, and virtual physics simulation, and combined theory with hands-on practice.

Activity 3 — ROS Basics & Joint Mechanics

We began by learning the fundamentals of ROS, which is a framework that helps the different parts of a robot work together. We explored how the various components of a robotic system, such as sensors, controllers, and motors, communicate with each other by exchanging messages, allowing complex robots to be built from smaller, independent programs. We also studied joint concepts and basic robotic-arm mechanisms, learning how joints connect the links of a robot and allow it to move. Finally, we studied joint kinematics, which describes how the movement of each joint affects the position and orientation of the robot, and is essential for controlling robotic arms accurately.

Activity 4 — Ubuntu & Turtlesim Movement

Next, we worked with Ubuntu, a Linux operating system, as the environment for running ROS. We learned basic command-line interface (CLI) commands and workflows, such as navigating folders, running programs, and managing files from the terminal. We then used Turtlesim, a simple ROS simulator in which a turtle moves around a window. By writing ROS commands, we controlled the turtle's movement and coordinates, and used it to draw the letters of our names on the screen. This activity was a fun and practical way to understand how ROS commands are sent to control the motion of a robot.

Activity 5 — Gazebo Vehicle Simulation

In the final activity, we worked with the Gazebo simulator, a 3D simulation tool commonly used with ROS. We ran and simulated our vehicle prototype in a virtual physics environment, where effects such as gravity, friction, and collisions are modelled realistically. Simulating the vehicle allowed us to observe how it would behave and move before building or testing it physically. This showed me how simulation helps engineers test ideas, find problems early, and save time and cost in the development of robotic systems.

Overall, Week 8 was a valuable learning experience that combined practical project planning with an introduction to robotics software. Finalising the electrical components moved our project prototype closer to the hardware build, while the robot selection activity taught me how to make engineering decisions under budget and scenario constraints. The ROS session gave me hands-on experience with robotics communication, joint kinematics, Ubuntu and the command line, Turtlesim, and Gazebo simulation, giving me a strong foundation for working with robotic systems.
